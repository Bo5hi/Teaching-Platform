Post-Incident Review Summary: INC-2026-001

Notes from the lessons-learned meeting on 28 April 2026, with the action tracker as of 1 September 2026. Provided by James Chen on request.

Fictional scenario for training. All names, companies, and addresses are made up.

In short

After the April 2026 break-in, Northgate held a meeting to review what happened and what to fix. This is the summary James wrote, plus the list of actions and their status. It is useful for two things: it is evidence of a lessons-learned process, and each action gives you something to check against the other evidence.

What happened (short timeline, US Central time)

WhenWhat
About 7 AprilMaria Rivera received a phishing email and entered her password on a fake login page. Her account did not have MFA.
15 April, 02:14Someone logged in to ng-files01 as mrivera from 198.51.100.77, using SSH through an old port-forward on the firewall.
15 April, early morningThe attacker became root through a badly set sudo rule, created a backdoor account support2, added a cron job to keep access, zipped /srv/shares/finance, and copied it to 192.0.2.200.
15 April, about 07:30James saw unusual outbound traffic in the firewall's daily bandwidth email and started to investigate.
15 April, 07:48 to 11:00James removed the port-forward, disabled mrivera and support2, removed the cron job, and reset passwords. Linda was told at 08:15.
16 Aprilsudo rule fixed. Maria enrolled in MFA. Finance team told to watch for fake bank-change requests.

Data affected

The finance share: vendor invoices and payment records, including bank account details for about 140 vendors. After legal advice, Northgate notified these vendors on 15 May 2026.

Why it happened (root causes agreed in the meeting)

  1. A password was stolen by phishing, and MFA was not required.
  2. SSH on the file server was reachable from the internet through a forgotten port-forward, even though policy said "VPN only".
  3. A misconfigured sudo rule let a normal user become root.
  4. Nothing alerted on the new account or the cron job. The outbound traffic was only seen hours later.
  5. There was no written incident response plan, so people were not sure who to call (insurance, lawyer).

What went well

  • James noticed within about 5 hours and contained the attacker the same morning.
  • Backups were not touched, and no systems needed to be rebuilt.
  • Staff reported two more phishing emails the same week.
